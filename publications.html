Opening the Black Box: Developing a Transparent Framework for Processing Consumer Smartphone/Wearable Data in Health Research (COMING SOON)

Smartphones and wearable devices count our steps every day, and researchers increasingly use these records to study health. But before the data can be analyzed, researchers have to make many hidden choices, such as how to handle repeated records or what to do when a smartphone and a watch disagree. These choices are rarely reported, yet they can change the results. We developed a framework that makes each choice visible and easy to report. Using data from people who had knee replacement surgery, we show that different reasonable choices can lead to different answers to the same question.

Impact of different levels and patterns of missing data on patterns of treatment response: a simulation study (COMING SOON)

People respond to treatment in different ways: after knee replacement, for example, some recover their activity quickly, others slowly. Smartphones and wearable devices can follow this recovery day by day, which helps reveal these different patterns of response. But people do not always carry their phone or wear their device, so many days of data go missing. This study asks how much missing data, and what kind of missing data, can be tolerated before the patterns of treatment response we find become misleading. We created simulator generated data for people recovering from knee replacement, so the true patterns were known. We then removed data at different levels, from a few days to almost all of them, and in different ways: at random, mostly early or late in recovery, or more often on days when people were less active. We then checked how well a standard statistical method could still find the true patterns. The results will help researchers judge when their findings can be trusted.

How to think about ‘representativeness’ in population health studies using smartphones and wearables – and what to do about it (COMING SOON)

Mobile consumer devices such as smartphones and wearables offer significant opportunities for advancing population health research by providing real-time, objective data about disease, its causes and its consequences. However, unequal device ownership and usage due to digital literacy and connectivity - linked to factors like age and socioeconomic status - can limit participation. This resource is an article consisting of a worksheet as well as empirical examples designed to help researchers consider representativeness when carrying out population health research using smartphones and wearables, reducing the potential for bias to affect final results.
